### The father / origin story

My first encounter with dictation came when I joined my father's law firm.

My father had told me that the law is something practiced by people based on their legal reasoning and experience. And that it was of questionable ethics to charge clients on a billable time basis for typing on a keyboard when that was slower in almost all cases than dictating, and the firm employed staffers who weren't billed to clients by the hour for more routine work including transcription. His point was that I could do all advanced legal work and no document processing or word processing.

He had begun his practice during the era of typewriters, and I'm not sure that he ever learned to type. For many years, he had relied on me for my advice in regards to even simple computer use — although I suspect he knows much more than he lets on. But, as he pointed out, his desk was free of a computer for quite some time.

I'll note that there are probably exceptions to this generalization, and that he may not have meant that all lawyers should prefer dictation in all cases. He was a litigator, as am I, and he may have not been thinking about the benefits of a computer for viewing and editing, say, lengthy contracts with multiple attachments and cross-referencing.

Although I respected my father, I didn't necessarily believe him at first.

### The race — D. Stasnopoulos

The proof of the power of dictation for me came at the end of the day one day when my father had left and so had most of the attorneys on the fourth floor of our office. The only ones remaining were myself and an old battle-worn [false start: "dicta—"] litigator named D. Stasnopoulos.

D. popped his head in my door and said that he would race me. Specifically, he said that he would be done and go home before I did, even though the discovery responses he had to draft were much lengthier than mine.

I was a fast typer, so I thought his hope would be dashed by my speed. I was barely a quarter of the way through when D. said goodbye to let me know he was heading home, having completed his work.

### D.'s two strategies (both revisited later in the book)

**First — the pre-built objection library.** He had already prepared a list of model objections that he would use over and over again in objecting to discovery responses. There are only about 10 or 15 such responses that are routinely used. So he attached a pre-written draft with the audio file of his dictation that would go to the word processor. And in his dictation, he referred to the objection. He'd say, for instance, "In response to interrogatory number 23, insert objections 2, 5, and 8." And then he'd be done with that interrogatory and moving on to the next. It took him seconds at most. Whereas my answers being typed were taking me minutes at best.

**Second — forced organization.** For substantive responses, because he was dictating, he had been forced to organize them very effectively and have them at a finger's reach so that he could pull and quote from documents, essentially reading. That kind of forced organization for the purpose of compiling dictation in one sitting is a genuine secret to speedy and effective dictation that will serve you well throughout your life in many areas beyond dictation.
